Understanding the problem

Waste awareness starts with knowing where waste comes from, what harm it can cause, and what practical action reduces that harm.

What counts as waste

Biodegradable/organic, recyclable, residual, e-waste, and sanitary or hazardous material — each needs different handling, which is why segregation at the source matters.

Why plastic persists

Plastic is cheap, light and durable — but much of it is used for minutes and discarded. It doesn't biodegrade like organic matter; it fragments into microplastics instead.

Human behaviour drives it

Buying habits, convenience culture, and disposal practices shape the waste stream as much as the materials themselves — on a campus as much as anywhere.

Environmental impact

Poorly managed plastic waste moves from streets and drains into rivers and oceans, fragmenting into microplastics and affecting wildlife along the way.

Health concerns

Unsafe handling, open burning, and waste near food or water sources create real but manageable risks — the fix is safe segregation and proper collection, not panic.

E-waste specifically

Old phones, chargers and laptops shouldn't go in ordinary waste. Wipe personal data first, then use an authorised e-waste collection channel.

Where tech students fit in

A smart system is only useful if it solves a real operational problem — observe how waste is generated and collected before choosing a technology.

IoT smart bins

Fill-level sensors report bin status to a dashboard so collection routes get planned by data instead of guesswork.

AI-assisted sorting

Computer vision can classify paper, plastic, metal or organic waste — as an aid alongside human oversight, not a replacement for it.

Data & dashboards

Anonymous surveys and waste counts turn an awareness campaign into something measurable, before and after.

Experiments & projects

Buildable ideas, ordered roughly from lightest to most involved. Each one can stand alone or feed into the "Smart Campus Waste Awareness and Monitoring System" as one working piece.

Sensor simulation (no hardware)

Simulate bin fill-levels in a spreadsheet or small script and drive a dashboard from it. The whole system's logic, provable before you touch a single sensor.

QR code sorting guide

A QR code on each bin links to a one-page guide for what goes where. Pairs directly with the Sort Guide concept on this page.

Anonymous waste survey

Run the sample questions from the brief, present results as a couple of charts, and write a one-paragraph interpretation of the biggest awareness gap.

Refill-station bottle tracker

A simple counter or form logging bottles refilled instead of bought — the kind of small, visible metric that makes a campaign feel real.

IoT fill-level sensor

A microcontroller and an ultrasonic or IR sensor estimate how full a bin is and push the reading to a small server or dashboard.

Computer-vision waste sorter

Train a small image classifier on a labelled set of common campus waste items, photographed as they actually appear — dirty, crushed, partly hidden.

E-waste collection drive

Plan and run a one-day authorised e-waste drop-off: intake form, a simple log of what came in, and a short report on volume collected.

Before/after reduction study

Follow the five-phase plan from the brief — observe, measure, act, add technology, evaluate — and report whether the intervention actually changed behaviour.
